Most of us think a 15x P/E is cheap and 80x is crazy expensive. But in real life, many 12x stocks stay stuck for years, while some 70x to 85x stocks go on to double or triple.

That's because a P/E isn't decided by the sector name. It depends on the type of business, its stage of life, its return on capital (ROCE), and how many years it can delay its growth slowdown.

These are the ideas that helped me understand it. Not investment advice, and I can be wrong.

## 1. The engine behind valuation: return on capital

ROCE is the profit a business makes on the money used in it. At 30% ROCE, every ₹100 put in earns ₹30 a year.

Take two companies. Both earn ₹100 crore a year and both grow profit 15%. So both need ₹15 crore of extra profit next year.

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | 12% | 30% |
| Money needed for ₹15 crore more profit | ₹125 crore (15 ÷ 12%) | ₹50 crore (15 ÷ 30%) |
| What's left from this year's ₹100 crore | Nothing. It borrows ₹25 crore more | ₹50 crore of free cash |

A has to build big plants or buy heavy equipment. It uses all its profit and still takes debt, just to grow. B grows at the same speed and still has ₹50 crore for dividends, buybacks or more expansion without debt.

That's why big investors happily pay 40x to 70x for a business like B, and only 10x to 14x for one like A.

## 2. Stage of life matters more than the sector name

Two companies in the same industry can trade at very different P/Es, depending on where they are on their growth curve.

The same is true for a whole industry in different countries. Contract drug manufacturing is mature in Switzerland and the US, where large players like Lonza grow steadily. In India it's still early, with a small share of the global market and plants being doubled. So Indian CDMOs trade at several times Lonza's P/E, as I've covered in [why Indian CDMOs trade so high](/blog/indian-cdmo-valuations/). A global peer comparison only helps when the industry is at the same stage in both places.

So I look at the type of business and its stage together. The ranges below are rough.

| Type and stage | Rough P/E | Why the market pays this | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital heavy cyclicals (mature, commodity) | 8 to 15 | Profit swings with global commodity prices. Heavy ongoing spending leaves little free cash. ROCE around 10% to 14% | Steel, metals, airlines |
| Lenders (mature, regulated) | 12 to 22 | Growth needs constant new capital. Hit by credit cycles and interest rate changes | Banks, NBFCs |
| Large defensive compounders (mature) | 20 to 35 | Big base, steady 8% to 12% growth, demand holds in a recession, good cash payouts. This creates a floor | Sun Pharma, Lonza |
| Asset light cash machines (mature, near monopoly) | 45 to 65 | Moderate 8% to 12% growth, but high ROCE, brand pricing power and most profit paid as dividends. People treat it almost like a safe bond | Nestlé India, HUL |
| High ROCE capacity builders (early, fast expansion) | 65 to 95 | Small market share with many years of runway, ROCE around 20% to 30%+, sticky contracts, and spending upfront to double capacity | Indian CDMOs like Anthem and Divi's, niche software firms |

### How to tell the stage from the numbers

The idea comes from Aswath Damodaran, the NYU professor known for valuation. In [his corporate life cycle work](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/the-corporate-life-cycle-managing), a company moves from start-up to high growth to mature to decline. He reads the stage from operating numbers: young firms show high revenue growth with large reinvestment, mature ones show large profits and free cash flow, and declining ones show pressure on growth and margins.

What I check, from five to ten years of numbers on any screener:

| What to look at | Early, still expanding | Mature | Declining |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales growth | High, often 15%+ | Steady, around the economy | Flat or falling |
| Margins | Rising | Stable | Under pressure |
| Capex against depreciation | Much higher. It's building | Close. It's maintaining | Lower. It's shrinking |
| Free cash flow | Low or negative | Strong | Falling |
| Dividends and buybacks | Little | A big share of profit | Rising as growth fades |
| Market share | Small, lots of room | Large, little room | Losing share |

He also says no single multiple fits every stage. Young companies get priced on revenue or users, mature ones on earnings, and declining ones closer to book value. That's why comparing P/Es across stages misleads.

## 3. Why "PEG = 1" fails for quality companies

PEG is P/E divided by growth. The textbook rule says PEG should be 1. So when a mature company slows to 12% growth, its P/E should fall to 12x.

In reality, good mature companies often settle at 25x to 30x even at 10% to 12% growth. Here's why.

A company with 30% ROCE needs to put back only 12 ÷ 30 = **40% of its profit** to grow 12%. The other **60% comes to shareholders** as cash. A debt free business with steady demand that pays out 60% of its profit is worth more than 12x.

The Gordon Growth Model puts this in one line:

**P/E = (1 − growth ÷ ROCE) ÷ (return you want − growth)**

This is a P/E version of the [value driver formula in McKinsey's *Valuation*](https://www.fm-magazine.com/news/2022/aug/value-meaning-measurement/), the standard book used by corporate finance teams. The "growth ÷ ROCE" part is the share of profit that has to go back in. McKinsey's own condition applies too: growth adds value only when the return on capital is higher than the return investors want.

With 30% ROCE and 12% growth, it gives (1 − 0.4) ÷ (0.14 − 0.12) = **30x** if you want 14% a year, and 25x if you want about 14.4%. Not 12x.

## 4. Why a PEG of 3 or 4 can also make sense

People say "a forward PEG above 2 is always overvalued". PEG misses three things.

1. **How long growth lasts.** Growing 24% for 2 years and growing 24% for 10 years give the same PEG today. But 24% for 10 years makes profit **8.6 times**.
2. **How much money growth needs.** To grow 24%, a 30% ROCE business puts back 80% of its profit (24 ÷ 30) and still has 20% spare. A 12% ROCE business needs 200% (24 ÷ 12), so it keeps borrowing.
3. **The P/E at the end.** After 10 years, a high ROCE business with sticky customers can settle around 34x. A 12% ROCE business settles around 10x. That's more than three times the end value for the same profit.

### The two-stage formula

**Justified P/E today ≈ ((1 + fast growth) ÷ (1 + return you want))^years × P/E at the end + cash paid out along the way**

- **Fast growth:** say 24% a year
- **Years:** how long the fast growth lasts before the company matures
- **Return you want:** say 11.5% a year
- **P/E at the end:** from the Gordon formula above, using ROCE and growth after maturity (9.5% here)

Plugging in:

| Business | Growth | Years | ROCE | P/E at the end | Justified P/E today | Forward PEG |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average capital heavy | 24% | 10 | 12% | 10x | 11x | 0.4 |
| High ROCE, short run | 24% | 2 | 30% | 34x | 43x | 1.4 |
| High ROCE, long run | 24% | 10 | 30% | 34x | 103x | 3.4 |
| High ROCE, very long run | 22% | 15 | 30% | 34x | 141x | 5.2 |

Forward PEG here is P/E on next year's earnings divided by growth.

Same 24% growth, same 10 years, yet one deserves 11x and the other 103x. **PEG = 1 thinks only about the next couple of years. When a 30% ROCE company compounds at 24% for 10 years and ends at 34x, the maths gives around 100x. So a PEG of 3 or 4 isn't automatically a bubble. It's just compounding over time.**

But it's sensitive. Keep everything else the same and change one input, and the 103x becomes:

- 66x if growth is 18%, not 24%
- 64x if growth after year 10 is 8%, not 9.5%
- 60x if fast growth lasts 5 years, not 10
- 53x if you want 13% a year, not 11.5%

## 5. When can ~100x be justified?

Not for a whole sector. Only for a small set of high ROCE capacity builders, and only when four things are true together.

1. **A long reinvestment runway at 30%+ ROCE.** Growth = ROCE × share of profit reinvested. 30% × 80% = 24% a year, without debt. If that lasts 10 years and the stock ends at 30x, paying about 85x to 90x today still gives around 11% to 12% a year.
2. **A small base in a big market.** Indian CDMOs together hold only 2% to 3% of a global market of about $145 billion. A small company can double or triple from tiny share gains, so the slowdown can be pushed out 10 to 15 years.
3. **Spending done upfront.** When a company is building a plant to double capacity, today's P/E divides a forward looking price by today's smaller profit. If the plant fills up, profit jumps, and an 85x to 100x P/E today can look like 45x to 55x on earnings two or three years out.
4. **High switching costs.** Nobody pays 100x for a business whose customers can leave tomorrow. Where changing a supplier takes years of approvals, like regulated CDMOs or critical enterprise software, revenue behaves almost like a long annuity.

### Justifiable isn't the same as safe

- **Around 65x to 85x**, there is still room for upside if the company beats expectations.
- **Around 95x to 100x and above**, there's no margin of safety. Every plant launch, every audit and every client trial has to go right. Even a small delay usually means a sharp 20% to 30% fall, or years of a flat stock price while earnings catch up.

## 6. Work backwards: the reverse DCF

Big funds don't guess whether 85x or 150x is too high. They work backwards. Michael Mauboussin and Alfred Rappaport made this popular in [*Expectations Investing*](https://www.fool.com/investing/2022/01/19/expectations-investing-qanda-mauboussin-rappaport): start from the price you know, and work out what it expects from the business. A simple version works backwards from year 10:

1. Assume the stock cools to a mature **30x** in year 10
2. Assume they want **11% to 12%** a year
3. Find the profit growth needed

| P/E today | Profit growth needed, every year for 10 years |
|---|---|
| 75x | About 22% to 23% |
| 85x | About 23% to 24% |
| 150x | About 30% to 32% |

For a debt free, 30% ROCE company doubling its factories, 23% for ten years is tough but possible. Above 30% for ten straight years, with no mistakes, in a business with physical factories, is very hard to believe. That's where big funds stop buying.

### Should it always be 10 years?

No. Ten is just a round number. Every company eventually slows to normal growth. As Damodaran puts it, the question is [not whether but when](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/highgrowthperiod.htm). So the number of fast years should come from the company itself.

**How Damodaran decides the length.** He looks at three things:

1. **Size against its market.** Smaller firms can keep earning high returns for longer, as long as the total market is also growing.
2. **Current growth and returns.** Momentum matters. A company growing fast and earning well above what investors want usually keeps doing it for a few years.
3. **Barriers to entry.** He calls this the most important one. Strong barriers, like long approvals or customers who can't switch, mean a longer period. Weak or fading barriers mean you should be much more careful.

Putting that together with the stage:

| Stage | Fast growth period I'd allow |
|---|---|
| Early, small share of a big market, strong barriers | 10 to 15 years |
| Growing, but the runway is visibly shorter or barriers are weak | 5 to 7 years |
| Mature | None. Value it directly with the steady-state formula |
| Cyclical | Don't stretch years. Use average earnings across a full cycle |

**A quick way to size the runway.** Ask how big the company could reasonably get, and how long that takes at its growth rate. If a company could grow its sales about 6 times before its share of the market looks stretched, at 20% a year that takes about 10 years.

**Better: let the price tell you the years.** Instead of assuming a period, Mauboussin and Rappaport, in [*Expectations Investing*](https://www.fool.com/investing/2022/01/19/expectations-investing-qanda-mauboussin-rappaport), solve for it. Damodaran calls this the [market-implied competitive advantage period](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/highgrowthperiod.htm): how long the company must keep earning high returns to justify today's price. Then you judge if that length is believable.

With 24% growth, an 11.5% return and a P/E of 30 at the end, a price of 85x needs about **10 years** of 24% growth.

**Growth doesn't stop overnight.** Real companies slow down step by step. If growth fades from 24% to 9.5% over 5 years instead of dropping at once, 85x needs about **8 fast years plus 5 slowing years**, around 13 years before growth becomes normal. Either way, the question is the same: can it really keep going that long?

### Re-checking after a year

Don't automatically roll a fresh 10 years forward every year. That quietly assumes the runway never gets shorter. And don't just subtract one either. I re-estimate from scratch.

Take the 85x stock a year later, with earnings up 24%:

| Price after a year | New P/E | Years of 24% growth now needed |
|---|---|---|
| Up 11.5%, exactly the return wanted | 76x | About 9. The clock simply ticked |
| Flat | 68x | About 8. It got cheaper against its runway |
| Up 40% | 96x | About 11. The market now assumes a longer runway |

If the year brought real evidence, like a new plant, new products or new customers, keeping or extending the runway can be fair. If nothing new happened but the price needs more years than before, the stock has become more demanding without a reason.

### The 10 year model is the base case, not the worst case

The model assumes ten smooth years before the P/E cools to 30x. From 85x, three things can happen:

| Scenario | Growth and ROCE | The stock |
|---|---|---|
| **Real worst case: execution fails** | A regulatory warning, a product failure or half empty factories. ROCE falls from 30% to around 14% | The P/E drops to 25x to 30x in year 2 or 3, not year 10. Even if profit still grows 15% a year, the price falls roughly 45% to 60% |
| **Base case** | Smooth execution, around 23% growth at 30% ROCE, then 30x in year 10 | You get your 11% to 12% a year |
| **Delayed cool-off** | New waves of demand keep growth at 25% or more, so the market still pays 50x to 55x in year 10 | About 18% to 22% a year. This is how the great compounders beat every 10 year model |

### What return can I expect?

John Bogle, who founded Vanguard, split stock returns into [three parts](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2016/09/the-john-bogle-expected-return-formula/): dividend yield, earnings growth, and the change in P/E. He called the first two the investment return and the last one the speculative return, because it depends on what people will pay later.

For one stock over a few years, I use the compounded version:

**Yearly return ≈ (1 + earnings growth) × (P/E at the end ÷ P/E today)^(1 ÷ years) − 1**, plus the dividend yield.

Say a stock is at 40x and grows earnings 20% a year for 5 years:

| P/E after 5 years | Return a year |
|---|---|
| Still 40x | About 20% |
| 30x | About 13% |
| 20x | About 4.5% |

The growth is the same in all three rows. Only the speculative part changes. Bogle said this part is the hardest to predict, so I don't guess it from today's mood. I pick the P/E at the end from the stage the company will probably be in by then. A company that will be mature in five years should be valued at a mature company's P/E, not at today's.

## 7. The second S-curve: when a mature stock re-rates

A company grows slowly, then fast, then flattens, like the letter S. Even after it matures and cools to 25x or 30x, it isn't stuck forever. If it finds a big new market or technology, earnings and the P/E rise together.

- **Eli Lilly** was a regular pharma giant, with a P/E [mostly between about 15x and 35x](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/LLY/LLY/pe-ratio) in the late 2010s. GLP-1 drugs for diabetes and obesity speeded up its growth, and its [three year average P/E reached about 67x](https://www.trefis.com/articles/601404/lly-stock-where-compounding-could-take-the-price/2026-06-04).
- **Microsoft** traded at [about 11.5x in 2012](https://www.aol.com/2012/04/28/how-cheap-is-microsofts-stock-by-the-numbers/). Azure cloud brought growth back, and its P/E later went above 30x.

Damodaran lists Microsoft in 2013, Apple in 2000 and IBM in 1992 as companies that [found a second life](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/the-corporate-life-cycle-managing). But he calls them the exception rather than the rule, and says luck played a part too. So I don't pay for a second curve upfront. I wait until it shows in sales and orders.

## What I take from this

I don't judge a stock by its P/E or its industry name alone. I ask three things:

1. What stage of life is it in?
2. How much cash does it make on every ₹100 invested?
3. How long can its tailwinds delay the P/E cool-off?
