Most of us start from the wrong end. A friend tells us a stock name, it's already up 40%, we buy it, and only after that we go looking for reasons. Then it falls 20% and we have no idea what to do.

I still struggle with this myself, especially with how much money to put where. So this is just how I think about it today. It's not investment advice, and I can be wrong.

[![Flow diagram of the investing journey: safety first, risk, asset split, market, themes, industry, stock screening, research, thesis, valuation, sizing, buying, tracking against an index and exit](/images/research/investing-framework.svg)](/images/research/investing-framework.svg)

To make it easier, take one imaginary person, Aman. He's 27, works in IT, spends around ₹50,000 a month, has ₹13 lakh saved and no big goal as of now.

## 1. First, keep some money out of the game

Aman puts ₹3 lakh, which is six months of expenses, in an FD or liquid fund. He also takes health insurance and clears his credit card dues. Now he has **₹10 lakh** to invest.

Boring, I know. But suppose the job goes in the same month the market crashes. This money is what saves him from selling at the bottom. And when a goal like buying a house comes up, that goal's money slowly moves to safer places.

## 2. How much fall can you actually handle, in rupees?

Everybody is a long-term investor in a rising market. Funny thing is, many people become one in a falling market also, even traders. The trade is deep in loss, booking it means accepting you were wrong, so quietly it becomes "long term".

This is a known habit. In [a study of 10,000 US brokerage accounts](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html), people were about 1.5 times as likely to book a profit as a loss. If I'm holding because my reasons are still true, that's investing. If I'm holding only because I can't digest the loss, I'm just stuck.

And falls can be big. The Nifty 50 fell around 60% in 2008 and around 38% in early 2020. A percentage doesn't hit as hard as rupees, so I ask it in rupees. Aman's honest answer is that he can watch **₹2.5 lakh** disappear on the screen without panic selling.

## 3. The split comes out from that number

I assume equity can fall around 45% in a bad phase, and the rest more or less holds. So ₹2.5 lakh divided by 45% gives about **₹5.5 lakh** for equity. The remaining ₹4.5 lakh goes into debt, gold and some waiting cash.

Waiting cash needs a rule from day one, something like "if the index falls 15% from its top, I put in one third". Otherwise it just keeps waiting forever.

I also like keeping some money outside India. One catch here: if you hold more than USD 60,000 in US shares or ETFs, [US estate tax](https://groww.in/blog/us-estate-tax-for-indian-investors) can become a problem for your family after you.

About timing, I don't try to guess where Nifty will be next year. I only see whether the market is costlier than usual, where interest rates are going, and whether suddenly everyone around me has become an expert. If it's too hot, I buy slowly. If people are scared, I look harder.

## 4. Funds first, then stocks

Aman is still learning, so 80% of his equity goes to funds and only 20% to direct stocks.

| Where | Amount |
|---|---|
| Nifty 500 index fund | ₹3.3 lakh |
| One flexi cap fund | ₹1.1 lakh |
| Direct stocks | ₹1.1 lakh |

Why not put everything in active funds? Over 10 years to mid 2025, [around 73% of large cap funds and 82% of mid and small cap funds did worse than their benchmark](https://cafemutual.com/news/industry/35917-73-of-indian-large-cap-and-82-of-mid-and-small-cap-funds-have-underperformed-sp-india-benchmarks-over-a-10-year-period). Also, check what the funds are holding before adding one more. Four funds owning the same companies is basically one fund.

## 5. Themes, where FOMO hits the hardest

A theme is some change that keeps running for years, like data centres, defence, renewables, or more household savings going into financial products. Your own job is actually a good place to spot one.

The problem is that every few months there is a new "hot sector". Some Budget announcement, some government push, some reel. If we put fresh money into each one, 4 sectors become 8, and then we can't track anything. A Value Research study of 61 sector and theme funds found that [investors earned much less than the funds themselves](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/224046/sectoral-funds-hot-returns-cold-reality/), with gaps of up to about 11 points a year, mostly because money came in after the rally.

So I keep a **parking list**:

| Date | Sector | Why it's in the news | Price then | Proof I'm waiting for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Today | Green hydrogen (example) | Government announcement | Note it down | Orders and sales showing in results |

Nothing goes from news to portfolio on the same day. And a new sector has to be better than the weakest one I already hold. If it is, it replaces that one.

## 6. Pick an industry, and decide how much

"Renewable energy" is too big to be an idea. Break it into solar, modules, cells and so on. Then the real question is **who is actually making money**, and usually it's whoever sits where the shortage is.

Some free places to study an industry:

- **IPO documents.** The "Industry Overview" chapter in a recent company's DRHP often carries a research firm's report.
- **Investor presentations and earnings calls** of the top three companies, available on their websites and exchange filings.
- **[RBI's sector-wise credit data](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/Data_Sectoral_Deployment.aspx)**, to see who is borrowing to grow.

### How much money in one industry?

I start from the index. In the Nifty 500, auto was [about 7.5% as of July 2026](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/ind_nifty_500.pdf), IT about 6.4% and financials about 31%. If I have no special view, the funds are already covering it.

If I do have a view, direct stocks add money **over and above** that. But first I score the bet:

| Question | 1 means | 5 means |
|---|---|---|
| Do I understand it? | I know only the headlines | I know who earns and why |
| How long can it grow? | A year or two | Many years |
| Is it showing in numbers? | Only talk | Orders, sales and cash going up |
| How costly is it? | Priced for perfection | Still reasonable |
| How badly can it fall? | Can become half quickly | Demand is steady |

| Score out of 25 | Target for that industry |
|---|---|
| 21 or more | Index weight + up to 10 points |
| 16 to 20 | Index weight + up to 5 points |
| 15 or less | Nothing extra, only watch |

If understanding itself is 2 or less, I skip it. For very small industries, I stop at around double the index weight.

**Say Aman picks auto.** It scores 18, so the target is 7.5 + 5 = **12.5% of equity**, which is ₹68,750. His ₹4.4 lakh in funds already has around ₹33,000 in auto, assuming the funds hold it like the index. So he needs to buy only about **₹36,000** directly.

| Industry | Index | Through funds | Score | Target | Buy directly |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Auto | 7.5% | 6% | 18 | 12.5% | About ₹36,000 |
| IT | 6.4% | 5.1% | 12 | No bet | Nothing |
| Financials | 31% | 24.8% | Not scored | No bet | Nothing |

Three things became clear to me from this calculation:

- **All bets together can't be more than the direct stock money.** Aman has 20% of equity for stocks and auto takes 6.5 of it. There's room for one or two more, so the "too many good sectors" problem solves itself.
- **Where I don't buy anything, I'm a little below the index**, because funds are only 80% of equity. That's fine, as long as I'm choosing it.
- **Connected bets count as one.** Auto makers, tyre companies and parts makers will fall together.

One last check. If auto can fall 50% and he's okay losing 10% of equity on it, the maximum he can put is 20%. He's well within that.

## 7. Shortlist in ten minutes

Any stock screener will do this (field names may differ a bit):

```
Market Capitalization > 500 AND
Sales growth 3Years > 15 AND
Average return on capital employed 3Years > 15 AND
Debt to equity < 0.5 AND
Pledged percentage < 5
```

This is only a filter. Banks and lenders need different numbers, and cyclical companies look their best right at the peak.

## 8. Research the ones that are left

1. Who is paying this company, and why only them?
2. What will a competitor need to beat them?
3. Did management deliver what they promised two years back? Old earnings call transcripts tell a lot.
4. Is profit actually turning into cash?
5. How much money does it need to grow? A business earning 30% on its capital needs far less money for the same growth than one earning 12%, and that changes [what P/E it deserves](/blog/how-stock-valuations-work/).
6. What can go badly wrong?

I've also put my research method into an AI skill, [data-backed stock analysis](/research/skills/data-backed-stock-analysis/).

## 9. Write it down before buying

| | ABC Auto Parts (imaginary) |
|---|---|
| Why I'm buying | More of its parts going into each vehicle, and rivals take years to get approvals |
| What must happen | Sales growth 17%+, margin 14%+, new orders 1.5 times of sales, positive operating cash |
| I'm wrong if | Margin stays under 11% for two results, a major customer leaves, or promoter pledges shares |
| Confidence | 60% |
| Sell date | None |
| Next check | Coming result |

There's no target price and no sell date. I hold as long as the reasons stay true, and I keep checking them. Rakesh Jhunjhunwala [held Titan for about two decades](https://www.businesstoday.in/personal-finance/investment/story/why-rakesh-jhunjhunwala-backed-titan-when-few-did-raamdeo-agrawal-explains-the-winning-bet-539515-2026-06-27) while it grew from watches into jewellery. But for every Titan there are many long holds that lose money, so the checking matters.

Cyclicals like metals or sugar are a different game. There you're betting on the cycle, and new supply ends it.

## 10. Costly stock? Find out what the bet is

A high P/E means many people are betting on something. I try to find that bet and see whether I believe it.

Suppose ABC (all numbers imaginary) is trading at **₹500** and earned **₹20 per share**. So P/E is **25**, while its usual is **20**. These two are facts. The only thing I estimate is future earnings.

### Why are people paying more?

I look at which questions analysts keep asking in the last few earnings calls. That shows what everyone is excited about. For ABC, it's more parts per vehicle and export orders coming in.

### How much can earnings grow?

Nobody knows exactly, so I check from four sides and take a range.

| Check | How | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| Past | Sales growth of the last 5 years | 16% |
| Management | They promise 22%, usually deliver 80% of it | About 18% |
| Pieces | Vehicle sales 8% + parts per vehicle 7% + market share 3% | About 19% |
| Capacity | 80% used today, new plant adds 35%, maximum use 90%. So sales can be 1.5 times in 3 years | About 15% |

So **sales can grow 15 to 19%**. If margin moves from 14% to 15%, that adds around 2%, so **earnings can grow 17 to 21%**. If the plant gets delayed or needs heavy debt, this story breaks, however good it sounds.

### Today's P/E and future P/E

The price stays ₹500. Only the earnings below it change. At 19% growth:

| | Today | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share | ₹20 | ₹24 | ₹28 | ₹34 |
| P/E at ₹500 | 25 | 21 | 18 | 15 |

Next year's 21 is still a little above the usual 20, so it's not cheap. The 15 in year three is my reward for waiting, but only if the growth actually comes. The bigger this gap between today's P/E and future P/E, the more I'm depending on growth.

Next year's P/E divided by growth, the forward PEG, comes to about **1.1**. I compare it with close competitors. A high PEG isn't automatically wrong either: a high ROCE business that keeps growing fast for ten years can justify 3 or 4, as I've [worked out here](/blog/how-stock-valuations-work/#4-why-a-peg-of-3-or-4-can-also-make-sense). For lenders, I'd look at price to book against return on equity.

### What can change the story?

Bad side first: the plant gets delayed, car sales slow down, a rival gets approved, or electric vehicles make its parts useless. Good side: a big export order or a new large customer, which pushes estimates up. For each of these I note one early sign to watch, like plant spending, monthly vehicle sales or export share.

Also ask what is **already expected**. If everyone is expecting that export order, getting it may not move the stock much, but missing it can crush it.

### When the P/E keeps going up

People pay more for a few reasons: they expect faster or longer growth, the business has become better (higher returns, less debt), there are very few listed options in a theme, money is flowing in, or it's plain excitement. The first three can last. The last two reverse quickly.

To tell them apart, suppose the price goes from ₹500 to ₹700 while earnings go from ₹20 to only ₹22. P/E jumps from 25 to 32. Now look at next year's estimate:

| Next year's estimate | Future P/E | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Raised from ₹24 to ₹29 | 24 | Much of the rise has a reason |
| Still ₹24 | 29 | Same earnings, only a costlier price tag |

Nobody can tell whether it will keep rising. What I can check is whether the reason is still alive. Warning signs for me are: price going up but estimates flat, P/E far above its own past and its peers, growth slowing, competitors adding capacity, lots of share sales happening in that sector, or promoters selling.

A rising P/E alone is not a reason to sell. If estimates are also rising, I hold. If they're flat, I stop adding. If warnings keep piling up, I sell a part.

## 11. How much in one stock

**Money I'm okay losing on this stock ÷ how much it can fall.**

Aman is okay losing 4% of his equity, which is ₹22,000. If ABC can fall 50%, his limit is **₹44,000**. But his room for auto is ₹36,000, which is lower, so that becomes the limit.

There's no magic number of stocks. Having mostly direct stocks and only a handful of them is risky. If funds are doing the heavy lifting, fewer is fine. Just don't own more than you can keep track of.

## 12. "By the time I'm convinced, it's too late"

This is a real problem when you have a job and limited time. So the money goes in as the knowledge goes up:

| Stage | What Aman has done | Money in |
|---|---|---|
| Trial | Quick check of business, debt, pledge and price | ₹10,000 |
| Thesis | Filled the table in step 9 | ₹13,000 more |
| Proof | One or two results support it | ₹13,000 more |

The trial has a deadline. If he hasn't done the work by the next result, he sells. Otherwise he'll end up with 25 small stocks bought in a hurry.

Even better is to study two or three areas while nobody is talking about them, and note down the price at which you'd buy. If an idea works only when you reach first, it's a trade, and people with jobs usually lose that race.

Average down only if the reasons still hold and just the price has fallen. No borrowed money and no F&O here. SEBI found that [more than 91% of individual F&O traders lost money in FY25](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/net-losses-of-traders-in-fo-widens-in-fy25-sebi-study-125070701221_1.html).

## 13. Track it without making it a second job

I check only the numbers I had written down. Say this is Aman's position after one result:

| Expected | Actual | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Sales growth 17%+ | 19% | Fine |
| Margin 14%+ | 12% | Watch |
| New orders 1.5x of sales | 1.7x | Fine |
| Positive cash | Yes | Fine |

One number missed, so he **stops adding**.

For each industry I watch three things: demand, supply and price. For auto parts that means vehicle sales, new capacity and margins, and P/E against its five year average.

| What you see | What to do |
|---|---|
| One of the three turns bad | Stop adding |
| Two turn bad, or one stays bad for two results | Cut the extra above index weight |
| "I'm wrong if" happens | Exit |
| All fine, price is fair | Add up to your limit |
| All fine, price is stretched | Hold, put new money somewhere else |

Holding 20 stocks? Read full results and calls for the big and risky ones, and do a quick number check for the rest. If you can't write why you own something, write it this quarter or sell it.

When I need to shift money, I use fresh money first, because jumping in and out is costly. In a [well-known US study](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/Returns.html), the households that traded the most earned 11.4% a year when the market gave 17.9%.

### Compare the whole money first

I compare everything against a **benchmark mix** which has the same split as mine. Here are one year's imaginary returns for Aman:

| Part | Money | Compared with | Benchmark | His return | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index fund | ₹3.3 lakh | Nifty 500 TRI | 12% | 11.8% | −₹660 |
| Flexi cap | ₹1.1 lakh | Nifty 500 TRI | 12% | 14% | +₹2,200 |
| Direct stocks | ₹1.1 lakh | Nifty 500 TRI | 12% | 8% | −₹4,400 |
| Debt | ₹2.5 lakh | Short term debt index or FD | 7% | 7.1% | +₹250 |
| Gold | ₹1 lakh | Gold price in rupees | 10% | 10% | 0 |
| Cash | ₹1 lakh | Liquid fund index | 6.5% | 6.5% | 0 |
| **Total** | **₹10 lakh** | **Benchmark mix** | **10.0%** | **9.74%** | **−₹2,610** |

His funds are doing their job, it's the stocks pulling him down. One year doesn't prove or disprove skill, but it shows where to look.

TRI means total return index, so dividends are included. If you buy mid or small caps, use the Midcap 150 or Smallcap 250, and for US money use the S&P 500 in rupees. Since money goes in on different dates, compare using XIRR on the same dates.

### Then each industry bet

Here there are two questions: was the industry right, and was my company right within it? Say Nifty 500 gave 12%, [Nifty Auto](https://www.niftyindices.com/indices/equity/sectoral-indices/nifty-auto) gave 18% and Aman's auto stocks gave 14% (imaginary again). The industry call was good, but his stock picking was 4 points behind. A simple auto index fund would have done better.

| Sector vs Nifty 500 | My stocks vs sector | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ahead | Ahead | Both calls are working |
| Ahead | Behind | Right industry, weak pick. An index fund may be enough |
| Behind | Ahead | Good company, but wrong industry or too early |
| Behind | Behind | Check the thesis again |

## 14. When to sell

- The reasons are gone, or were wrong from day one
- Something doesn't feel right about the management
- The price now needs a miracle
- There's a clearly better idea, even after paying tax
- It has become too big a part of your money
- You need the money for a goal

Price falling or rising alone isn't a reason. Warren Buffett [sold all his airline stocks](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/05/02/what-warren-buffett-said-at-berkshires-2020-shareh.aspx) in 2020 because the business itself had changed.

## Is all this research even worth it?

One good year can be luck, and you can't keep waiting forever to find out. But your thinking gets tested at every result. So I note one row for each idea: date, why, expected numbers, how confident I am, price, index level and hours spent.

| | Stock did well | Stock did badly |
|---|---|---|
| **Reasons came true** | Good call | Check if you paid too much |
| **Reasons failed** | You got lucky | Your mistake, note why |

Be careful with "got lucky". It feels exactly like skill and makes you bet bigger.

After every 10 ideas or so, compare them with the previous 10. Are more of the expected numbers coming true? When you said 70% sure, did around 7 out of 10 work? Are you breaking your own rules less? And are your stocks beating the benchmark after costs and tax, counting the losers you already sold? Also keep an eye on what you rejected or sold. If those are doing better, something is wrong with your filter.

| What you see | What I'd do |
|---|---|
| Learning is improving, returns near or above index | Put a little more into stocks |
| Learning is improving, returns still behind | Keep the stock share same |
| No improvement, or mostly luck | Go back to funds |
| No time, or too much stress | An index fund and a couple of good funds. Totally fine |

Do the money math also. On ₹5 lakh, beating the index by 3% means ₹15,000 a year, which is small for all those hours. The real thing you're building is a skill for bigger money later. If that skill isn't improving, you're not getting much out of it.
